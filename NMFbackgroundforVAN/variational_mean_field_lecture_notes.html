1. General Framework: Variational Free Energy

We follow the notation of Wu, Wang & Zhang (arXiv:1809.10606), referred to throughout as the VAN paper. The key idea is: we cannot compute the partition function $Z$ exactly for interesting models, so we introduce a tractable approximate distribution $q_\theta(s)$ and minimize a variational upper bound on the true free energy.

1.1 The Boltzmann distribution and the partition function

Consider a system of $N$ binary spins $s = (s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_N)$ where each $s_i \in \{+1, -1\}$. The joint probability follows the Boltzmann distribution:

$$ p(s) \;=\; \frac{e^{-\beta E(s)}}{Z}\,, \qquad Z \;=\; \sum_{s} e^{-\beta E(s)}\,, \tag{1} $$

where $\beta = 1/(k_B T)$ is the inverse temperature, $E(s)$ is the energy of configuration $s$, and $Z$ is the partition function. The sum $\sum_s$ runs over all $2^N$ configurations.

The free energy is:

$$ F = -\frac{1}{\beta} \ln Z = -k_B T \ln Z\,. \tag{2} $$

Computing $F$ exactly requires summing over exponentially many configurations — a $\#$P-hard problem in general.

1.2 The variational ansatz $q_\theta(s)$

We introduce a variational distribution $q_\theta(s)$, parameterized by some set of variational parameters $\theta$. The idea: we will choose $q_\theta$ from a tractable family of distributions, and adjust $\theta$ so that $q_\theta$ is as close as possible to the true Boltzmann distribution $p$.

The closeness is measured by the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence:

$$ \KL(q_\theta \,\|\, p) \;=\; \sum_s q_\theta(s)\, \ln \frac{q_\theta(s)}{p(s)} \;\geq\; 0\,. \tag{3} $$

$\KL = 0$ if and only if $q_\theta(s) = p(s)$ for all $s$.

1.3 KL divergence and the variational free energy $\Fq$

Substituting $p(s) = e^{-\beta E(s)}/Z$ into (3):

$$ \KL(q_\theta \| p) = \sum_s q_\theta(s) \left[\ln q_\theta(s) - \ln p(s)\right] = \sum_s q_\theta(s) \left[\ln q_\theta(s) + \beta E(s) + \ln Z\right]. $$
Since $\sum_s q_\theta(s) = 1$: $$ \KL(q_\theta \| p) = \sum_s q_\theta(s)\left[\beta E(s) + \ln q_\theta(s)\right] + \ln Z = \beta\!\left(\underbrace{\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_s q_\theta(s)\left[\beta E(s) + \ln q_\theta(s)\right]}_{\displaystyle \Fq}\right) - \beta F\,. $$

This gives us the fundamental variational relation (VAN paper, Eq. 2):

$$ \boxed{ \KL(q_\theta \| p) \;=\; \beta\bigl(\Fq - F\bigr) \;\geq\; 0 } \tag{4} $$

where the variational free energy is (VAN paper, Eq. 3):

$$ \Fq \;=\; \frac{1}{\beta}\sum_s q_\theta(s)\bigl[\beta E(s) + \ln q_\theta(s)\bigr] \;=\; \avg{E}_q + k_B T\avg{\ln q_\theta}_q\,. \tag{5} $$

We can write this more transparently as:

$$ \Fq \;=\; \underbrace{\sum_s q_\theta(s)\, E(s)}_{\text{average energy } \avg{E}_q} \;-\; T\,\underbrace{k_B\!\Bigl(-\sum_s q_\theta(s) \ln q_\theta(s)\Bigr)}_{\text{thermodynamic entropy } S_q}\,. \tag{6} $$

That is,

$$ \Fq = \avg{E}_q - T\, S_q\,, \qquad S_q = k_B\, \mathcal{H}_q\,, \tag{7} $$

where we have introduced the Shannon entropy (dimensionless, measured in nats):

$$ \mathcal{H}_q \;\equiv\; -\sum_s q_\theta(s)\,\ln q_\theta(s)\,, \tag{7a} $$

and the thermodynamic (Gibbs) entropy $S_q = k_B \,\mathcal{H}_q$, which carries the dimensions of $k_B$ (energy/temperature).

Shannon entropy vs. physics entropy

Throughout these notes we use $\mathcal{H}$ for the dimensionless Shannon entropy and $S = k_B \mathcal{H}$ for the physical entropy. The relation is simply:

$$ S_{\text{physics}} = k_B \times \mathcal{H}_{\text{Shannon}}\,. $$

Many machine-learning references (including the VAN paper) set $k_B = 1$, so that $S$ and $\mathcal{H}$ coincide and $\beta = 1/T$. Most physics textbooks keep $k_B$ explicit. We carry it here so you can compare directly with both conventions. When you see a physics reference writing $S = -k_B \sum p \ln p$, or an ML reference writing $H = -\sum p \ln p$ with $\beta = 1/T$, they are saying the same thing.

Key point
Since $\KL \geq 0$, we have $\Fq \geq F$. The variational free energy is an upper bound on the true free energy. Minimizing $\Fq$ over $\theta$ tightens this bound, making $q_\theta$ approximate $p$ as closely as possible within the chosen family.

1.4 The naïve mean field (NMF) ansatz

The simplest variational family is a fully factorized distribution:

$$ q_\theta(s) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{N} q_i(s_i)\,, \tag{8} $$

where each $q_i(s_i)$ is a Bernoulli distribution over $s_i \in \{+1, -1\}$. Since each $q_i$ must be normalized, it is characterized by a single parameter. Let us parameterize it via the local magnetization:

$$ m_i \;\equiv\; \avg{s_i}_q \;=\; \sum_{s_i = \pm 1} s_i\, q_i(s_i) \;=\; q_i(+1) - q_i(-1)\,. \tag{9} $$

Combined with normalization $q_i(+1) + q_i(-1) = 1$, we get:

$$ q_i(s_i) \;=\; \frac{1 + m_i\, s_i}{2}\,, \qquad m_i \in [-1,\, +1]\,. \tag{10} $$

So the variational parameters are $\theta = (m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_N)$.

Physical meaning
The factorized ansatz says: the spins are statistically independent. Each spin $s_i$ has its own probability of being up or down, but there are no correlations between different spins. This is the defining approximation of naïve mean field theory. The VAN paper generalizes this by replacing the product of independent marginals with a product of conditional probabilities — the autoregressive decomposition — which can capture correlations.

1.5 Evaluating $\Fq$ under the NMF ansatz

We need to compute the two pieces of $\Fq$: the average energy and the entropy.

Entropy

Since $q_\theta(s) = \prod_i q_i(s_i)$ and the spins are independent under $q$, the Shannon entropy factorizes:

Step 1: $$ \mathcal{H}_q = -\sum_s q_\theta(s) \ln q_\theta(s) = -\sum_s \prod_j q_j(s_j) \sum_i \ln q_i(s_i)\,. $$
Step 2: In the sum over all configurations, the product factorizes. For each term $i$, the sums over all $s_j$ with $j \neq i$ just give 1 (normalization), leaving only the sum over $s_i$: $$ \mathcal{H}_q = -\sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{s_i=\pm 1} q_i(s_i) \ln q_i(s_i) \;\equiv\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathcal{H}(q_i)\,, $$
where $\mathcal{H}(q_i)$ is the binary Shannon entropy of the $i$-th marginal. Substituting $q_i(s_i) = (1+m_i s_i)/2$:
$$ \mathcal{H}_q = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathcal{H}(m_i)\,, \qquad \mathcal{H}(m) \equiv -\frac{1+m}{2}\ln\frac{1+m}{2} - \frac{1-m}{2}\ln\frac{1-m}{2}\,, \tag{11} $$

and the thermodynamic entropy is $S_q = k_B\,\mathcal{H}_q = k_B \sum_i \mathcal{H}(m_i)$.

Note that $\mathcal{H}(0) = \ln 2$ (maximum uncertainty, $\sim 0.693$ nats) and $\mathcal{H}(\pm 1) = 0$ (fully determined spin).

The binary Shannon entropy $\mathcal{H}(m)$. Maximum at $m = 0$ (complete uncertainty about spin direction); zero at $m = \pm 1$ (spin fully determined). This is the entropy per spin in nats; multiply by $k_B$ for the thermodynamic entropy per spin.

Average energy

The average energy depends on the specific model. We keep things general here and specialize in Sections 2–3. For any energy that is a sum of pairwise interactions and local fields,

$$ E(s) = -\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle} J_{ij}\, s_i s_j - \sum_i h_i\, s_i\,, $$

the factorized ansatz gives (since $\avg{s_i s_j}_q = \avg{s_i}_q \avg{s_j}_q = m_i m_j$ for $i \neq j$):

$$ \avg{E}_q = -\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle} J_{ij}\, m_i\, m_j - \sum_i h_i\, m_i\,. \tag{12} $$
This is the key simplification
Under the factorized ansatz, the two-point correlation $\avg{s_i s_j}_q$ factorizes into $m_i m_j$. This is the mathematical content of "replacing interactions by an effective mean field." It is exact within the variational distribution $q$ (the spins really are independent under $q$), but it makes $q$ a poor approximation to $p$ whenever the true correlations matter.

Putting it together

The variational free energy for the general pairwise model is:

$$ \Fq = -\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle} J_{ij}\, m_i m_j - \sum_i h_i m_i - k_B T \sum_{i=1}^N \mathcal{H}(m_i)\,. \tag{13} $$

1.6 Minimizing $\Fq$: the NMF self-consistency equations

We minimize $\Fq$ with respect to each $m_i$. Setting $\partial \Fq / \partial m_k = 0$:

Energy derivative: $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial m_k}\left(-\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle} J_{ij} m_i m_j - \sum_i h_i m_i\right) = -\sum_{j \in \partial k} J_{kj}\, m_j - h_k\,, $$ where $\partial k$ denotes the neighbors of site $k$.
Entropy derivative: $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial m_k}\bigl(-k_B T\, \mathcal{H}(m_k)\bigr) = -k_B T \cdot \frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial m_k} = -k_B T \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1+m_k}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1-m_k}{2}\right)\,. $$ This simplifies using $\tanh^{-1}$: $$ \frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial m_k} = -\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1+m_k}{1-m_k} = -\tanh^{-1}(m_k)\,. $$ So the entropy contribution is $+k_B T\,\tanh^{-1}(m_k)$.
The entropy derivative $\partial\mathcal{H}/\partial m = -\tanh^{-1}(m)$, which acts as the entropic "restoring force" in the NMF self-consistency equation. It diverges logarithmically as $m \to \pm 1$ (resisting full polarization) and has slope $-1$ at the origin (dashed red line). The NMF fixed point occurs where this entropic term balances the energetic driving term $\beta\sum_j J_{kj} m_j$.
Setting to zero: $$ -\sum_{j\in\partial k} J_{kj}\, m_j - h_k + k_B T\,\tanh^{-1}(m_k) = 0\,. $$

Solving for $m_k$:

$$ \boxed{ m_k = \tanh\!\left(\beta\Bigl[\sum_{j\in\partial k} J_{kj}\, m_j + h_k\Bigr]\right) } \tag{14} $$

These are the naïve mean field (NMF) self-consistency equations. There is one equation for each site $k$. The term in brackets is the effective field experienced by spin $k$:

$$ h_k^{\mathrm{eff}} = \sum_{j\in\partial k} J_{kj}\, m_j + h_k\,. \tag{15} $$

Each spin sees its neighbors only through their mean magnetization $m_j$ — hence the name "mean field."

Comparison with the VAN framework
In the VAN paper, the NMF ansatz $q_\theta(s) = \prod_i q_i(s_i)$ is the simplest special case of the autoregressive ansatz $q_\theta(s) = \prod_i q_\theta(s_i | s_1,\ldots,s_{i-1})$. In NMF, each conditional $q_\theta(s_i | s_{<i})$ is independent of $s_{<i}$; it collapses to $q_i(s_i)$. The VAN paper's contribution is to parameterize the conditionals with neural networks, allowing correlations while retaining a tractable, normalized $q_\theta$.

2. Application: 1D Ising Model (Periodic Boundary Conditions)

2.1 Setup and energy function

Consider a ring of $N$ spins with nearest-neighbor ferromagnetic coupling $J > 0$ and no external field ($h = 0$). The energy is:

$$ E(s) = -J \sum_{i=1}^{N} s_i\, s_{i+1}\,, \qquad s_{N+1} \equiv s_1 \quad \text{(periodic BCs)}\,. \tag{16} $$

Each site has exactly $z = 2$ neighbors (left and right). There are $N$ bonds in total (the periodic bond $s_N s_1$ gives the $N$-th bond).

2.2 NMF variational free energy for the 1D chain

We assume a uniform (translation-invariant) solution: $m_i = m$ for all $i$. This is natural given the periodic boundary conditions and the translation symmetry of the Hamiltonian. Then:

Average energy

From Eq. (12) with $J_{ij} = J$ for neighboring pairs and $h_i = 0$:

$$ \avg{E}_q = -J \sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i\, m_{i+1} = -J \cdot N \cdot m^2\,. \tag{17} $$

(There are $N$ bonds, each contributing $-J\, m^2$.)

Entropy

From Eq. (11), the Shannon entropy is $\mathcal{H}_q = N\,\mathcal{H}(m)$, so the thermodynamic entropy is:

$$ S_q = k_B\, N\, \mathcal{H}(m) = -k_B N\left[\frac{1+m}{2}\ln\frac{1+m}{2} + \frac{1-m}{2}\ln\frac{1-m}{2}\right]. \tag{18} $$

Variational free energy

Combining via $\Fq = \avg{E}_q - T S_q$, and dividing by $N$ to get the free energy per site $f_q \equiv \Fq / N$:

$$ f_q(m) = -J m^2 + k_B T\left[\frac{1+m}{2}\ln\frac{1+m}{2} + \frac{1-m}{2}\ln\frac{1-m}{2}\right]. \tag{19} $$
Structure of $f_q(m)$

The first term ($-Jm^2$) favors large $|m|$ — it is the energy gain from alignment. The second term (the entropy cost, with coefficient $+k_BT$) favors $m = 0$ — it is maximized when the spin is equally likely up and down. The competition between energy and entropy is the essence of the phase transition physics.

2.3 Deriving the NMF equations

We minimize $f_q(m)$ by setting $df_q/dm = 0$:

Step 1: Differentiate $$ \frac{df_q}{dm} = -2Jm + k_B T \cdot \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1+m}{1-m} = -2Jm + k_B T\,\tanh^{-1}(m)\,. $$
Step 2: Set to zero and solve $$ k_B T\,\tanh^{-1}(m) = 2Jm \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad m = \tanh\!\left(\frac{2J}{k_B T}\, m\right) = \tanh(2\beta J\, m)\,. $$

This is the special case of the general NMF equation (14) for a uniform solution on the 1D chain. Each spin has $z = 2$ neighbors, each with magnetization $m$, so the effective field is $h^{\mathrm{eff}} = 2Jm$:

$$ \boxed{m = \tanh(2\beta J\, m)} \tag{20} $$
Note on the coordination number
The factor of 2 in $2\beta J m$ comes from the coordination number $z = 2$ (each spin has 2 neighbors on the periodic chain). In general dimension $d$ on a hypercubic lattice, $z = 2d$, and the NMF equation becomes $m = \tanh(z \beta J m)$ in the uniform case.

2.4 Solving for the phase transition

Equation (20) is a transcendental equation for $m$. We solve it graphically/analytically.

The graphical construction makes the bifurcation structure clear: below $T_c$, the sigmoid $\tanh(2\beta J m)$ is steeper than the diagonal at the origin, producing two additional fixed points at $\pm m_0(T)$. At $T = T_c$ the sigmoid is tangent to $y = m$ at the origin (slope $= 1$). Above $T_c$ only $m = 0$ survives.

$m = 0$ is always a solution (paramagnetic state).

Non-zero solutions exist when the slope of the RHS at $m = 0$ exceeds 1. Since $\frac{d}{dm}\tanh(2\beta J m)\big|_{m=0} = 2\beta J$, a bifurcation occurs when:

$$ 2\beta_c J = 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k_B T_c^{\text{NMF}} = 2J\,. \tag{21} $$

For $T < T_c^{\text{NMF}}$, there are two additional solutions $m = \pm m_0(T)$ with $m_0 > 0$, corresponding to a ferromagnetically ordered state. This is a continuous (second-order) phase transition within NMF.

Critical behavior near $T_c$

Expanding $\tanh(x) \approx x - x^3/3$ for small $m$ near $T_c$:

$$ m \approx 2\beta J\, m - \tfrac{1}{3}(2\beta J)^3 m^3 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad m^2 \approx \frac{3(2\beta J - 1)}{(2\beta J)^3}\,. $$
Near $T_c$, with $t \equiv (T_c - T)/T_c$ the reduced temperature: $$ m \sim |t|^{1/2}\,, $$ which gives the mean-field critical exponent $\beta_{\text{MF}} = 1/2$.

2.5 Comparison with the exact solution

NMF is qualitatively wrong in 1D!

The 1D Ising model with short-range interactions has no phase transition at any finite temperature. This was shown by Ising himself (1925) and can be proved exactly using the transfer matrix method. The exact free energy per site is:

$$ f_{\text{exact}} = -k_B T \ln\!\left(2\cosh\beta J\right) + \text{(boundary corrections that vanish as } N\to\infty\text{)}\,. $$

The magnetization is $m = 0$ for all $T > 0$ (in the absence of an external field). The correlation length is $\xi = -1/\ln(\tanh \beta J)$, which is finite for all $T > 0$.

NMF predicts a spurious phase transition at $k_B T_c = 2J$. This is because the factorized ansatz completely ignores fluctuations, which in 1D are strong enough to destroy long-range order at any nonzero temperature.

Why does NMF fail in 1D? The factorized ansatz $q(s) = \prod_i q_i(s_i)$ sets all correlations to zero: $\avg{s_i s_j}_q - m^2 = 0$. But in 1D, thermal fluctuations create domain walls (boundaries between up and down regions) at a free-energy cost of only $2J$ per wall, offset by an entropy gain of $\sim T \ln N$ from placing the wall anywhere on the chain. Since the entropy always wins for $T > 0$, the chain disorders. NMF misses this because it does not see domain walls — those are inherently correlated structures.

This is a well-known limitation discussed in many textbooks. The VAN paper demonstrates that more expressive ansätze (autoregressive networks) can correctly capture such correlations and avoid the spurious transition.

The NMF variational free energy $\mathcal{F}_q$ always lies above the exact free energy $F$ (it is less negative), as guaranteed by $D_{\text{KL}} \geq 0$. The two curves agree at low $T$ (where both approach $-J$, the ground state energy) and diverge most around $T \sim T_c^{\text{NMF}} = 2J$.

Where does the phase transition show up in $f_q$?

Since the NMF transition is second-order, $f_q(T)$ itself is continuous and its first derivative $-\partial f_q/\partial T = S$ (the entropy) is also continuous at $T_c$. The transition is invisible in the free energy curve! It only appears in the second derivative: the specific heat $C = -T\,\partial^2 f_q / \partial T^2$ has a jump discontinuity at $T_c$. Above $T_c$ the NMF gives $f_q = -k_BT\ln 2$ (a straight line, zero specific heat), while just below $T_c$ the ordered solution bends the curve, giving a finite $C$. This specific-heat jump $\Delta C = \frac{3}{2}k_B$ per spin is the classic mean-field result.

By contrast, the exact 1D free energy $f = -k_BT\ln(2\cosh\beta J)$ is analytic for all $T > 0$ — smooth to all orders, with no transition at all.

3. Application: 2D Ising Model on the Square Lattice (Periodic BCs)

3.1 Setup and energy function

Consider an $L \times L$ square lattice with $N = L^2$ spins and periodic boundary conditions (a torus). The energy is:

$$ E(s) = -J \sum_{\langle i,j\rangle} s_i\, s_j\,, \tag{22} $$

where $\langle i,j \rangle$ runs over all nearest-neighbor pairs. On the square lattice, each site has coordination number $z = 4$. With periodic BCs, there are $N_{\text{bonds}} = 2N$ bonds in total (each site contributes 2 bonds: one to the right and one downward).

3.2 NMF variational free energy for the 2D lattice

Again assuming a uniform solution $m_i = m$ for all $i$ (which respects the lattice translation symmetry):

Average energy

$$ \avg{E}_q = -J \sum_{\langle i,j\rangle} m_i m_j = -J \cdot 2N \cdot m^2\,. \tag{23} $$

(There are $2N$ bonds on the periodic square lattice, each contributing $-Jm^2$. Equivalently, each of the $N$ sites contributes $\frac{1}{2} z \cdot (-Jm^2) = -2Jm^2$ per site.)

Entropy

Exactly as before:

$$ S_q = k_B\, N\, \mathcal{H}(m)\,. \tag{24} $$

Variational free energy per site

$$ f_q(m) = -2J m^2 + k_B T\left[\frac{1+m}{2}\ln\frac{1+m}{2} + \frac{1-m}{2}\ln\frac{1-m}{2}\right]. \tag{25} $$
Comparison with 1D
The only difference from the 1D case (Eq. 19) is the coefficient of $m^2$ in the energy term: $-Jm^2$ (1D, $z=2$) vs. $-2Jm^2$ (2D, $z=4$). In general, for coordination number $z$, the energy per site is $-\tfrac{z}{2}Jm^2$. The entropy per site is the same: $-k_B\mathcal{H}(m)$.

3.3 Deriving the NMF equations

Minimizing $f_q(m)$ proceeds identically to the 1D case.

Differentiate: $$ \frac{df_q}{dm} = -4Jm + k_BT\,\tanh^{-1}(m) = 0\,. $$
Solve: $$ m = \tanh\!\left(\frac{4J}{k_BT}\, m\right) = \tanh(4\beta J\, m)\,. $$

This is the general result (14) specialized to the 2D square lattice with $z = 4$ neighbors:

$$ \boxed{m = \tanh(4\beta J\, m)} \tag{26} $$
General pattern
For any lattice with coordination number $z$ and uniform coupling $J$, the NMF equation in zero field is always: $$ m = \tanh(z\beta J\, m)\,, $$

with $\beta = 1/(k_BT)$. This follows directly from Eq. (14): each site has $z$ neighbors, each contributing $Jm$ to the effective field, giving $h^{\mathrm{eff}} = zJm$. The NMF critical temperature is $k_BT_c = zJ$.

3.4 Solving for the phase transition

The analysis is identical to Section 2.4 but with $z = 4$ instead of $z = 2$.

Bifurcation condition: $\frac{d}{dm}\tanh(4\beta J m)\big|_{m=0} = 4\beta J = 1$, giving:

$$ \boxed{k_B T_c^{\text{NMF}} = 4J} \qquad\text{(2D square lattice, NMF)}\,. \tag{27} $$

Below $T_c$: Two non-trivial solutions $m = \pm m_0(T)$ appear continuously from $m = 0$, indicating a second-order (continuous) phase transition to a ferromagnetic state.

Critical exponents: Near $T_c$, expanding as before gives $m \sim |t|^{1/2}$ with $t = (T_c - T)/T_c$. All NMF critical exponents take the classical mean-field values regardless of lattice or dimension:

$$ \beta_{\text{MF}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,, \quad \gamma_{\text{MF}} = 1\,, \quad \delta_{\text{MF}} = 3\,, \quad \alpha_{\text{MF}} = 0\,. \tag{28} $$

Landau expansion of $f_q(m)$

For small $m$, we can expand the Shannon entropy to get a Landau-type free energy. Using $\mathcal{H}(m) = \ln 2 - \frac{m^2}{2} - \frac{m^4}{12} - \cdots$:

$$ f_q(m) = -2Jm^2 - k_BT\left(\ln 2 - \frac{m^2}{2} - \frac{m^4}{12} - \cdots\right) $$ $$ = \text{const} + \frac{1}{2}(k_BT - 4J)m^2 + \frac{k_BT}{12} m^4 + \cdots $$
$$ = f_0 + \frac{a}{2}\, m^2 + \frac{b}{4}\, m^4 + \cdots \tag{29} $$ with $a = k_BT - k_BT_c = k_B(T - T_c)$ where $k_BT_c = 4J$, and $b = k_BT/3 > 0$.

This is precisely the Landau free energy. For $a > 0$ ($T > T_c$), the minimum is at $m = 0$. For $a < 0$ ($T < T_c$), minima appear at $m = \pm\sqrt{-a/b} = \pm\sqrt{3(4J - k_BT)/(k_BT)}$. The transition is continuous with $m \sim |a|^{1/2}$.

3.5 Comparison with the exact Onsager solution

The 2D Ising model on the square lattice was solved exactly by Onsager (1944). The exact results are:

Critical temperature:

$$ k_B T_c^{\text{exact}} = \frac{2J}{\ln(1+\sqrt{2})} \approx 2.269\, J\,. \tag{30} $$

Critical exponent for magnetization: $\beta_{\text{exact}} = 1/8$ (Yang, 1952).

NMF overestimates $T_c$ and gets the wrong exponents

NMF overestimates $k_BT_c$ by a factor of $\sim 1.76$. This is because by ignoring correlations, NMF overestimates the tendency toward ordering (every spin "thinks" it has $z = 4$ perfectly aligned neighbors, rather than fluctuating ones). As the VAN paper demonstrates (Fig. 2a), more expressive variational ansätze dramatically reduce this error.

However, NMF is qualitatively correct in 2D: it correctly predicts the existence of a phase transition and the existence of spontaneous magnetization below $T_c$. This is a significant improvement over the 1D case, where NMF gives a qualitatively wrong prediction. The general rule is:

  • In 1D: NMF is qualitatively wrong (predicts a transition where none exists).
  • In 2D: NMF is qualitatively correct but quantitatively poor.
  • In $d \geq 4$ (the upper critical dimension): NMF becomes exact for the critical exponents.

4. Summary: NMF as the Simplest Case of VAN

Let us place everything back in the VAN paper's framework. The paper defines the variational free energy for a general ansatz $q_\theta(s)$:

$$ \Fq = \frac{1}{\beta}\sum_s q_\theta(s)\left[\beta E(s) + \ln q_\theta(s)\right]. $$

Three levels of the ansatz are considered:

AnsatzForm of $q_\theta(s)$Correlations?Parameters
NMF $\prod_i q_i(s_i)$ None $N$ (one $m_i$ per site)
VAN (1 layer) $\prod_i q_\theta(s_i|s_{<i})$, conditionals parameterized by weights $W_{ij}$ Pairwise $N(N-1)/2$
VAN (deep) $\prod_i q_\theta(s_i|s_{<i})$, conditionals parameterized by deep network All orders $\sim 10^5$–$10^6$

The NMF ansatz is recovered from the autoregressive decomposition $q_\theta(s) = \prod_i q_\theta(s_i | s_1, \ldots, s_{i-1})$ by setting all the weight matrices $W$ to zero, so that each conditional $q_\theta(s_i | s_{<i})$ becomes independent of $s_{<i}$ and reduces to a site-dependent Bernoulli distribution $q_i(s_i)$.

4.1 Why 1-layer VAN has pairwise ($N(N-1)/2$) parameters

Let us spell out the structure of the simplest non-trivial VAN — the one-hidden-layer autoregressive network — to see exactly where the $N(N-1)/2$ parameters come from and why they capture pairwise correlations.

The autoregressive decomposition

By the chain rule of probability, any joint distribution can be written exactly as:

$$ q(s_1, \ldots, s_N) = q(s_1)\, q(s_2|s_1)\, q(s_3|s_1,s_2) \cdots q(s_N|s_1,\ldots,s_{N-1}) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} q(s_i | s_{<i})\,, $$

where $s_{<i} = (s_1, \ldots, s_{i-1})$. This is exact — no approximation yet. The key insight of VAN is to parameterize each conditional $q_\theta(s_i | s_{<i})$ with a neural network while preserving the autoregressive (causal) structure: the conditional for site $i$ may depend only on sites $1, \ldots, i-1$.

Each conditional is a Bernoulli distribution

Since $s_i \in \{+1, -1\}$, each conditional $q_\theta(s_i | s_{<i})$ is a Bernoulli distribution characterized by a single number — the conditional magnetization:

$$ \mu_i \equiv \avg{s_i}_{q(\cdot | s_{<i})} = \sum_{s_i = \pm 1} s_i\, q_\theta(s_i | s_{<i})\,. $$

A natural parameterization is through a sigmoid (hyperbolic tangent) activation applied to a linear function of the conditioning variables:

$$ \mu_i = \tanh\!\left(b_i + \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} W_{ij}\, s_j\right), \tag{32} $$

so that $q_\theta(s_i | s_{<i}) = (1 + \mu_i\, s_i)/2$. Here $b_i$ is a bias and $W_{ij}$ (for $j < i$) is the weight connecting the "input" $s_j$ to the "output" $\mu_i$. The autoregressive constraint is enforced structurally: $W_{ij} = 0$ for $j \geq i$ (site $i$ cannot see itself or future sites).

Counting the parameters

Now count the independent parameters in Eq. (32):

Biases: There is one bias $b_i$ for each site $i = 1, \ldots, N$. These play the same role as the NMF magnetization parameters $m_i$ — they set the "baseline" probability for each spin independent of the others. $$ \text{biases:} \quad N \text{ parameters}\,. $$
Weights: The weight $W_{ij}$ exists for every ordered pair with $1 \leq j < i \leq N$. The weight matrix $W$ is strictly lower-triangular: $$ W = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ W_{21} & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ W_{31} & W_{32} & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & & \vdots \\ W_{N1} & W_{N2} & \cdots & W_{N,N-1} & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$ The number of entries below the diagonal is: $$ \text{weights:} \quad 1 + 2 + \cdots + (N-1) = \frac{N(N-1)}{2} \text{ parameters}\,. $$
Total: The 1-layer VAN has $N + N(N-1)/2 = N(N+1)/2$ parameters in total. If we absorb the biases into the framework (or count only the "new" parameters beyond NMF), the weight parameters alone number $N(N-1)/2$.

Why this captures pairwise correlations

With the parameterization (32), the log-probability of a configuration is:

$$ \ln q_\theta(s) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \ln q_\theta(s_i | s_{<i}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \ln\!\frac{1 + \mu_i\, s_i}{2}\,. $$

Expanding the $\tanh$ inside $\mu_i$ to leading order in the weights $W_{ij}$ (which is exact when $W$ is small), we find:

For small $W$, $\mu_i \approx \tanh(b_i) + (1 - \tanh^2(b_i)) \sum_{j < i} W_{ij}\, s_j$, so the conditional distribution is approximately: $$ \ln q_\theta(s_i | s_{<i}) \approx \text{const}(b_i) + \alpha_i\, s_i + \sum_{j < i} \tilde{W}_{ij}\, s_i s_j + \cdots $$ where $\alpha_i$ depends on $b_i$ and $\tilde{W}_{ij}$ is proportional to $W_{ij}$. Summing over $i$: $$ \ln q_\theta(s) \approx \text{const} + \sum_i \alpha_i\, s_i + \sum_{i>j} \tilde{W}_{ij}\, s_i s_j + \cdots $$
This has exactly the form of a pairwise (Ising-like) model: effective local fields $\alpha_i$ and effective couplings $\tilde{W}_{ij}$ between every pair of spins. The $N(N-1)/2$ weights $W_{ij}$ map onto $N(N-1)/2$ pairwise couplings — one for each pair $(i,j)$.
NMF as the $W = 0$ limit

Setting all $W_{ij} = 0$ in Eq. (32) gives $\mu_i = \tanh(b_i)$, which is independent of $s_{<i}$. Then $q_\theta(s) = \prod_i q_i(s_i)$ with $m_i = \tanh(b_i)$, recovering exactly the NMF ansatz of Section 1. The biases $b_i$ are related to the NMF magnetizations by $b_i = \tanh^{-1}(m_i)$. Thus NMF sits at the origin of the VAN parameter space: the simplest, cheapest, but least expressive member of the family.

Beyond pairwise: deep VAN

Adding hidden layers to the network parameterizing $\mu_i$ allows the conditional to be a nonlinear function of $s_{<i}$, which generates effective multi-spin interactions (three-body, four-body, etc.) in $\ln q_\theta$. This is how deep VAN can capture the higher-order correlations needed near critical points — and why it dramatically outperforms NMF and even 1-layer VAN for the 2D Ising model near $T_c$.

The NMF equations (14) can be solved iteratively: start from a random initial guess $\{m_i^{(0)}\}$ and iterate

$$ m_k^{(t+1)} = \tanh\!\left(\beta \sum_{j \in \partial k} J_{kj}\, m_j^{(t)}\right) \tag{31} $$

until convergence. This is very cheap — each step is $O(N)$ — but the quality of the resulting $\Fq$ is limited by the factorized ansatz. The VAN paper shows that by using neural network parameterizations and gradient-based training (the REINFORCE algorithm, Eq. 5 of the VAN paper), one obtains dramatically better approximations to the free energy, especially near the critical point where correlations are strongest.
