9The DFT Matrix and Spectral Filtering

In the previous chapter we derived the eigenvectors of the shift operator $S$ — the columns of the DFT matrix. Now we assemble the full picture: the DFT as a matrix, and the spectral filtering recipe that makes convolution diagonal.

Let $\omega = e^{i2\pi/N}$ be a primitive $N$th root of unity. The DFT matrix $W$ has entries:

Eq. 15 — DFT Matrix
$$ W_{mk} \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\, e^{\,i\,2\pi mk/N} \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\,\omega^{mk}, \qquad m,k = 0,1,\ldots,N-1 \tag{15} $$

The row index $m$ labels the frequency and the column index $k$ labels the position. Each row of $W$ is a complex sinusoid at frequency $m$, sampled at positions $k = 0, \ldots, N-1$. The $1/\sqrt{N}$ normalization makes $W$ unitary: $W^*W = WW^* = I$, where $W^*$ is the conjugate transpose. The inverse is therefore:

Eq. 16 — Inverse DFT
$$ W^{-1} = W^* = \overline{W}^T, \qquad \text{or equivalently } (W^{-1})_{km} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\,\omega^{-mk} \tag{16} $$

With the unitary normalization, $W^{-1} = W^*$. Some texts use the convention without the $1/\sqrt{N}$ factor in $W$, placing all $N$ in the inverse: $W_{mk} = \omega^{mk}$, $W^{-1} = \frac{1}{N}W^*$. The physics is the same; only the bookkeeping differs.

Now recall the fundamental result from Chapter 2: every circulant matrix $C_h$ is diagonalized by $W$. This means convolution with any filter $h$ can be performed in three steps:

The Spectral Filtering Recipe

To compute $y = h * x$ (circular convolution of filter $h$ with signal $x$):

  1. Transform: $\hat{x} = Wx$ — decompose the signal into frequency components.
  2. Multiply: $\hat{y}_m = \hat{h}_m \cdot \hat{x}_m$ for each $m$ — scale each frequency independently.
  3. Invert: $y = W^{-1}\hat{y}$ — reconstruct the filtered signal.

In matrix notation, step 2 is multiplication by a diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(\hat{h})$, so the entire pipeline collapses to:

Eq. 17 — Spectral Filtering Formula
$$ y \;=\; W^{-1}\,\operatorname{diag}(\hat{h})\; W\, x \;=\; C_h\, x \tag{17} $$

This is the convolution theorem in matrix form. The circulant $C_h$ factors as $W^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(\hat{h})\,W$: conjugate into the frequency domain, apply a diagonal scaling, conjugate back. Convolution in the spatial domain is pointwise multiplication in the frequency domain.

Figure 3.1. The spectral filtering pipeline. A signal $x$ is transformed by the DFT matrix $W$, multiplied pointwise by the filter’s spectrum $\hat{h}$, then transformed back by $W^{-1}$. The result is identical to circular convolution $h * x$.

Why this matters for graphs: The spectral filtering formula $y = W^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(\hat{h})\,Wx$ does not mention the group $\ZN$ at all. It only requires an eigenbasis $W$ and a set of spectral multipliers $\hat{h}$. This is the formula that will generalize to graphs — we will simply replace $W$ with the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian.

10Three Isomorphic Algebras

We have been building three different descriptions of the same mathematical object. Now we state the precise relationship. The following theorem is the capstone of the $\ZN$ story.

Theorem 5.1 (Bamieh) — Three Isomorphic Algebras

The following three sets, each equipped with their natural operations, are isomorphic as algebras:

  1. Circulant matrices $\{C_h : h \in \C^N\}$ — the set of all $N \times N$ circulant matrices, with matrix multiplication.
  2. Convolution sequences $(\C^N, *)$ — the set of all $N$-vectors, with circular convolution as multiplication.
  3. Spectral multipliers $\{\operatorname{diag}(\hat{h}) : \hat{h} \in \C^N\}$ — diagonal matrices in the DFT basis, with matrix multiplication (= pointwise multiplication of spectra).

The word “isomorphic” means there are bijective maps between these three sets that preserve addition and multiplication. In concrete terms:

  • Embedding: $h \;\leftrightarrow\; C_h$ — any vector $h$ can be embedded as the first column of a circulant matrix, and conversely any circulant is determined by its first column.
  • Fourier transform: $h \;\leftrightarrow\; \hat{h} = Wh$ — the DFT maps convolution sequences to spectral multipliers.
  • Diagonalization: $C_h \;\leftrightarrow\; \operatorname{diag}(\hat{h})$ via $C_h = W^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(\hat{h})\,W$ — the DFT diagonalizes every circulant.
Eq. 18 — The Isomorphism
$$ \underbrace{\{C_h\}}_{\text{Circulant matrices}} \;\cong\; \underbrace{(\C^N, *)}_{\text{Convolution sequences}} \;\cong\; \underbrace{\{\operatorname{diag}(\hat{h})\}}_{\text{Spectral multipliers}} \tag{18} $$

The isomorphism means that any statement about one algebra translates directly into a statement about the others. Convolution of two sequences $h * g$ corresponds to multiplication of two circulants $C_h C_g = C_{h*g}$, which corresponds to pointwise multiplication of spectra $\hat{h} \cdot \hat{g} = \widehat{h*g}$. The three viewpoints are three windows onto a single algebraic structure.

Figure 3.2. The commutative triangle of the three isomorphic algebras. Any path between two vertices gives the same result: embedding then diagonalizing is the same as taking the DFT then forming the diagonal. This is Bamieh’s Theorem 5.1.

Physics parallel: This triangle is analogous to the position/momentum duality in quantum mechanics. A quantum state can be described equivalently in position space $\psi(x)$, momentum space $\tilde{\psi}(p) = \Ft[\psi](p)$, or as a matrix in some operator basis. The Fourier transform shuttles between representations, but the physics is representation-independent. Here, the “physics” is the convolution operation, and the three algebras are three representations of it.

11Why Everything Worked

Before we move to graphs, it is essential to understand why the $\ZN$ story was so clean. Three structural properties conspired to make everything work. Understanding them will tell us exactly what breaks on general graphs.

The Three Pillars of the ZN Story
  1. Group structure. $\ZN$ is a group (under addition mod $N$) that acts on the set of vertices $\{0, 1, \ldots, N-1\}$ by translation. Every vertex “looks the same” — the group acts transitively. This is the algebraic root of translation invariance: shifting a signal by $k$ positions is the group element $k$ acting on the signal.
  2. Canonical generator. The group $\ZN$ is cyclic, generated by the single element $1$. This gives a unique “shift by one” operator $S$, which in turn gives a unique notion of convolution: linear operators that commute with $S$. There is no ambiguity about which shift to use.
  3. Distinct eigenvalues. The shift operator $S$ has $N$ distinct eigenvalues: the $N$th roots of unity $\omega^0, \omega^1, \ldots, \omega^{N-1}$. Because all eigenvalues are distinct, each eigenspace is one-dimensional, and the eigenbasis is unique (up to scalar multiples).
Eq. 19 — Distinct Eigenvalues
$$ |\operatorname{spectrum}(S)| = N \quad\text{(all eigenvalues distinct)} \tag{19} $$

The distinctness of the eigenvalues is what made the commutant theorem so powerful. Because $S$ has $N$ distinct eigenvalues, the set of matrices that commute with $S$ is exactly the set of polynomials in $S$ — which is exactly the set of circulant matrices. The three characterizations (group-theoretic, algebraic, spectral) all coincide precisely because of this distinctness.

To summarize the logical chain:

  • Group structure $\Rightarrow$ a natural notion of “shift” and translation invariance.
  • Canonical generator $\Rightarrow$ a unique shift operator $S$, hence a unique algebra of convolutions.
  • Distinct eigenvalues $\Rightarrow$ a unique eigenbasis (the DFT), and the three characterizations coincide.

The punchline: Everything rested on three pillars: group structure, a canonical generator, and distinct eigenvalues. On graphs, we lose two of them.

12What Will Break on Graphs

Now we preview Part II of the course. Consider an arbitrary undirected graph — say, a social network, a molecule, or a finite-element mesh. What happens to our three pillars?

Pillar 1: Group structure — BROKEN. An irregular graph has no translation symmetry. There is no group acting transitively on its vertices. Vertex 0 might have degree 5 while vertex 1 has degree 2 — they are fundamentally different. The group-theoretic definition of convolution (“linear operators that commute with the group action”) simply does not apply.

Pillar 2: Canonical generator — BROKEN. With no group, there is no canonical shift operator. On a general graph, we will use the graph Laplacian $L$ (or the adjacency matrix $A$, or the normalized Laplacian $\tilde{L}$) as our “shift-like” operator. But this is a choice, not a necessity. Different choices lead to different notions of convolution.

Pillar 3: Distinct eigenvalues — OFTEN BROKEN. Real symmetric matrices (like $L$ and $A$) can have repeated eigenvalues. When they do, the corresponding eigenspace is multi-dimensional, and any orthonormal basis for that eigenspace works equally well. The eigenbasis is no longer unique.

Despite these losses, not everything dies. The key insight is that two of our three characterizations survive:

Property ZN General Graph
Group structure ✓ ZN acts on vertices ✗ No transitive group
Canonical generator ✓ Shift S ✗ Must choose (L, A, …)
Distinct eigenvalues ✓ (roots of unity) Often ✗ (repeated)
Algebraic char. (poly in operator) ✓ ✓
Spectral char. (diagonal in eigenbasis) ✓ ✓
Group-theoretic char. (commute with S) ✓ ✗
Eq. 20 — What Survives
$$ \underbrace{p(L) = \theta_0 I + \theta_1 L + \cdots + \theta_d L^d}_{\text{Algebraic: polynomial in }L} \qquad\longleftrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{U\,\operatorname{diag}(\hat{h})\,U^T\,x}_{\text{Spectral: diagonal in eigenbasis}} \tag{20} $$

The algebraic characterization — define convolutions as polynomials in a chosen operator — survives because we can always form polynomials in any matrix. The spectral characterization — define convolutions as diagonal in the operator’s eigenbasis — survives because any real symmetric matrix has a complete orthonormal eigenbasis (even with repeated eigenvalues, we can always choose one). The group-theoretic characterization is the one that genuinely dies, because an irregular graph has no group.

Subtlety ahead: On $\ZN$, the algebraic and spectral characterizations define the same set of operators. On graphs with repeated eigenvalues, the spectral class is strictly larger than the polynomial class. There exist spectral multipliers that are not polynomials in $L$. This gap will become important in Chapter 5 when we compare spectral and polynomial graph convolutions.

With this roadmap in hand, we are ready to leave the comfort of $\ZN$ and step onto general graphs. In the next chapter, we begin by symmetrizing the shift operator (forming $A = S + S^*$), see eigenvalue collapse in action, introduce the graph Laplacian $L$, and verify which characterizations survive.
